# **INTRODUCTION**

## **Situation Analysis**

Communication is central to a child's ability to learn, connect, and take part in everyday life, yet for many children on the autism spectrum, this foundational skill does not emerge without deliberate, sustained support. Speech sound difficulties are challenges in producing and organizing the sounds of language, a common co-occurring feature of autism spectrum disorder, and when left unaddressed, they tend to compound the social and academic barriers these children already navigate. Addressing these difficulties early and consistently is widely recognized as critical to long-term outcomes, yet the systems meant to provide that support remain constrained by limited clinical capacity, cost, and geographic access — constraints that this study positions technology to help ease.

For children on the autism spectrum who present with co-occurring speech sound difficulties, speech and communication delays remain among the most limiting barriers to daily interaction, and early, consistent intervention is widely regarded as essential to speech and language development. Access to that intervention, however, continues to fall short of demand. A systematic review of AI-based automated speech therapy tools reports that a considerable share of practicing Speech-Language Pathologists (SLPs) maintain waiting lists, a shortage that leaves many families without timely access to structured therapy (Deka et al., 2024). Traditional clinic-based therapy further compounds the problem, since sessions are typically brief and infrequent, leaving little room for the sustained, repeated practice that speech-sound acquisition requires, particularly for children who do not yet have consistent access to a licensed SLP because of cost, distance, long waiting lists, or the absence of a formal diagnosis.

A growing body of research has attempted to extend structured speech practice beyond the clinic through automatic speech recognition (ASR) and related technologies, and several of these efforts demonstrate that automated systems can approximate the judgment of a trained clinician. Kim et al. (2025), for instance, validated a fine-tuned ASR model against the transcriptions of three licensed SLPs on two standardized Korean articulation tests administered to thirty children with suspected speech sound disorders, reporting intraclass correlation coefficients above 0.97, although disagreement was more pronounced for misarticulated phonemes and liquid sounds. Consistent with this, Getman et al. (2023) fine-tuned a wav2vec2-based model on a corpus of children's speech that included children clinically diagnosed with Speech Sound Disorder, and found that the resulting system functioned reliably as a combined speech recognizer and pronunciation evaluator suitable for a gamified, mobile pronunciation-training application, performing close to human raters when evaluated using mean absolute error. Extending this evidence to more atypical speech patterns, Choi et al. (2026) found that several ASR systems achieved strong agreement with adult listeners' intelligibility ratings for children with dysarthria, suggesting that automated assessment can generalize beyond typically-developing speech to the atypical, harder-to-classify pronunciation patterns that children on the autism spectrum may present. Beyond validating accuracy against clinicians, recent work also shows that this level of performance is achievable directly on a mobile device rather than a remote server: Dutta et al. (2025) adapted a compressed version of OpenAI's Whisper model for lightweight, on-device recognition of children's speech, demonstrating that the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff of edge-deployed ASR has improved enough to support a genuinely capable, privacy-preserving mobile application. Reviewing this body of work specifically as it applies to autism, Mao and Zhu (2026) found that AI-powered speech recognition holds significant potential for supporting autism-specific speech-language intervention, but noted that no reviewed system integrates recognition, pronunciation assessment, and SLP-facing progress monitoring into a single application, the precise combination EchoVoice is designed to provide.

Despite this progress in raw recognition accuracy, existing systems consistently fall short in the areas of data privacy, clinician involvement, and rigorous validation. Desolda et al. (2021) developed Pronuntia, a web-based remote rehabilitation system built around three linked roles, the SLP, the caregiver, and the child, that allowed therapists to design personalized exercises, calibrate recognition thresholds, and monitor progress remotely while caregivers guided practice at home; usability testing with therapists and caregivers was largely successful, but the researchers acknowledged that the system's patient-authentication method was not convincing from a privacy standpoint and that children themselves were excluded from testing. At a broader level, Deka et al. (2024) reviewed twenty-four automated speech therapy tools for speech sound disorders and found that most were designed for high or full automation without sufficiently involving SLPs, parents, or caregivers in the process, and that only four of the twenty-four studies reviewed compared their tool's output against expert SLP evaluation at all. Usha and Alex (2023) reached a similar conclusion across a much larger review of eighty-eight studies, finding that automated speech-assessment tools remain inconsistent in their universality, reliability, and validity across languages and populations, and that the field's commonly accepted clinical-utility threshold, agreement of roughly 75 to 85 percent with human raters, has been met by only some of the tools reviewed. This validation gap is especially pronounced for autism specifically: Lee et al. (2024) noted that the field still lacks speech corpora and validated benchmarks built specifically for autistic children, which limits how confidently any automated system, including EchoVoice, can claim accuracy across the full range of pronunciation patterns this population may present, underscoring why EchoVoice keeps a licensed SLP as the final check on its automated scores rather than acting on them unilaterally.

Beyond the international evidence base, a growing line of Philippine research demonstrates that accurate, low-latency automatic speech recognition can be developed and deployed locally rather than relying on commercial cloud APIs, showing that the technical capacity and research foundation for on-device speech applications already exist within the Philippine research landscape this study is situated in. Ing, Pascual, and Dimzon (2022), for example, trained a hybrid Time Delay Neural Network-Hidden Markov Model (TDNN-HMM) system on the Filipino Children's Speech Corpus and achieved a word error rate as low as 0.97%, consistently outperforming a traditional HMM-GMM baseline across all tested configurations. Building on this line of work, Dorado and Villanueva (2023) developed a real-time Filipino children's ASR system that reached 98.51% accuracy with live child speakers while responding 1.4 times faster than Google Speech-to-Text and twice as fast as the Whisper API, directly demonstrating that low-latency, locally-trained recognition can outperform cloud-based alternatives for this population. Complementing these accuracy- and latency-focused studies, Malaay and Ventura (2024) developed a CNN-based ASR system for Filipino-English code-switching, or Taglish, speech, a linguistic pattern common in everyday Filipino conversation that any locally deployed therapy application must be able to accommodate; and Pascual et al. (2023) extended children's ASR development to a second major Philippine language, building baseline Filipino and Cebuano acoustic models for a healthcare-oriented application. None of these studies, however, moved beyond raw recognition benchmarking to build a deployable pronunciation-therapy application, provide phoneme-level error scoring, or involve children with speech sound disorders or autism specifically.

At the application level, two recent Philippine-context systems illustrate both the promise and the persistent limitations of AI-assisted speech therapy tools for Filipino children. Sijera et al. (2025) developed SamurAI, an AI-powered home-based speech therapy application built through a user-centered design process with practicing SLPs, and, notably, kept the clinician actively involved throughout development rather than treating automation as a full replacement for professional judgment; preliminary testing showed the system could transcribe speech, detect errors, and generate clinically recognized metrics such as the Percentage of Consonants Correct. However, SamurAI depends on OpenAI's GPT-4.0 for its transcription and error analysis, meaning every recording must be sent to an external cloud server, and the tool remains limited to English and to children aged four to seven, with Tagalog support left for future work, precisely the connectivity- and privacy-related limitations EchoVoice's offline architecture is designed to avoid. Similarly, Samonte et al. (2024) developed YugTalk, a Filipino-based augmentative and alternative communication application validated with real pediatric patients using standardized instruments such as the Preschool Language Scale-Fifth Edition, and reported measurable communication gains; but YugTalk operates entirely online through Firebase, functions as a communication board rather than a phoneme-level pronunciation assessment tool, and was evaluated with only three children over a short period. Taken together with the international literature discussed above, these Philippine-context studies confirm that the individual components EchoVoice will combine, capable local-language ASR, clinician-involved design, and validated therapeutic outcomes, already exist separately within the Philippine research landscape, but no reviewed system, local or international, integrates them into a single, fully offline, phoneme-level pronunciation assessment tool built with autistic children who present with co-occurring speech sound difficulties in mind.

Consistent with the scope adopted by Sijera et al. (2025) for SamurAI, EchoVoice's initial development and evaluation will focus on English-language speech targets, given the greater availability of validated English pronunciation references and assessment frameworks; expanding language coverage is identified as a direction for future work.

This study will address that gap through EchoVoice, a privacy-preserving mobile application that will be developed to extend structured speech practice into the home, usable by any family regardless of whether they currently have access to a Speech-Language Pathologist. Powered by a compressed, on-device foundation ASR model, EchoVoice will be designed to operate entirely offline, so that a child's voice recordings will never need to leave the device under any configuration and so that families in areas with unreliable internet access will not be excluded from its use. For families who are working with an SLP, EchoVoice will additionally allow caregivers to export a clinical-grade progress report covering phoneme-level accuracy trends, error patterns, and session history, which they may share with their clinician through their own preferred means, without requiring a server, an account, or continuous connectivity. By combining offline processing, optional clinician-ready reporting, and parental empowerment in a single mobile application, this study intends to move beyond speculative technology toward a validated, accessible tool that supports the speech development of autistic children with co-occurring speech sound difficulties, whether or not clinical care is currently within reach.

## **Statement of the Objectives**

The primary objective of this study is to design, develop, and evaluate EchoVoice, an on-device pronunciation assessment mobile application that extends privacy-preserving speech therapy practice into the homes of autistic children with co-occurring speech sound difficulties.

		Specifically, the study aims to achieve the following objectives: 

- To design and implement a compressed, on-device ASR model that delivers real-time, phoneme-level pronunciation scoring without an internet connection;

- To develop an on-device mobile application for pronunciation assessment practice, including an exportable progress report; and

- To evaluate the application's on-device feasibility on consumer-grade hardware and validate its scoring accuracy against the clinical judgments of a licensed SLP.

**Time and Place of the Study**

	This study will be conducted during the academic year 2026-2027 in partnership with Growth Journey Learning Center Inc., the researchers' identified clinical partner for the project, located at San Antonio, Agoo, La Union, where the EchoVoice application will be introduced, tested, and evaluated in coordination with the center's Speech-Language Pathologist and the families it serves. The research will be undertaken by Bachelor of Science in Computer Science students of the Don Mariano Marcos Memorial State University – South La Union Campus.

## **Definition of Terms**

For a better understanding of this research, the following terms are conceptually and operationally defined:

*Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) *refers to the compressed, on-device foundation model that EchoVoice will use to transcribe and analyze a child's spoken attempts at target words without transmitting audio off the device.

*Clinician-Ready Progress Report *refers to the downloadable summary EchoVoice will produce for caregivers, containing phoneme-level accuracy trends, error patterns, and session history, which caregivers may share with an SLP through whatever means they choose.

*On-Device Processing *refers to the design approach in which all of EchoVoice's speech recognition and pronunciation scoring will be performed locally on the child's mobile device, so that voice recordings never leave the device and the application can function without an internet connection.

*Phoneme-Level Pronunciation Scoring *refers to the automated assessment output EchoVoice will produce by evaluating a child's spoken attempt at the level of individual speech sounds rather than whole-word accuracy alone.

*Pronunciation Assessment *refers to the overall process by which EchoVoice will compare a child's recorded speech attempt against a target pronunciation and generate a corresponding accuracy score.

*Speech-Language Pathologist (SLP) *refers to the licensed clinician at the researchers' partner institution whose evaluations will serve as the external validation standard against which EchoVoice's automated scoring will be compared.

*Speech Sound Difficulties *refers to the articulation and pronunciation challenges presented by the autistic children who will serve as EchoVoice's target users, and which its listen-and-repeat exercises are designed to address through automated, phoneme-level practice.

*Speech Therapy *refers to the structured practice EchoVoice will extend into the home to complement clinician-led sessions, aimed at improving a child's speech sound production and overall communication ability.

**METHODOLOGY**

**Research Design**

	This study will employ Developmental Research, specifically a Type 1 developmental study, as its general design, following Seels and Richey's (1994) definition of developmental research as the systematic study of designing, developing, and evaluating instructional or technical programs, processes, and products against criteria of internal consistency and effectiveness. Type 1 developmental studies focus on a specific instructional or technical product, program, process, or tool, and typically address not only its design and development but its evaluation as well (Richey, 2005). This classification fits the study directly: EchoVoice's central output is a functional software artifact whose design, construction, and real-world feasibility form the primary object of investigation, evaluated across three phases that mirror the study's specific objectives — designing and implementing a compressed, on-device ASR model; developing the offline mobile interface for listen-and-repeat practice, including an exportable, clinician-ready progress report; and evaluating the application's feasibility on consumer-grade hardware while validating its scoring accuracy against the clinical judgment of a licensed SLP at the study's partner institution, Growth Journey Learning Center Inc.

To carry out this developmental design in practice, the study will adopt a Design Science Research (DSR) methodology (Nagle et al., 2022), an approach built specifically for research whose primary contribution is an artifact, such as a model, method, or system, created to solve an identified problem and then rigorously evaluated for its real-world utility. This approach is well established for software-based health interventions in particular: Sumarsono et al. (2022) applied the same DSR framework to develop and evaluate a mobile health infrastructure for non-communicable disease management, following a comparable build-then-evaluate structure to produce a deployable, empirically validated health application. EchoVoice's development will follow the same underlying logic, proceeding in two linked phases: a developmental phase, addressing Objectives 1 and 2, in which the application is designed and built according to its finalized system documentation; and an evaluative phase, addressing Objective 3, in which the completed application's scoring accuracy and on-device feasibility are formally assessed.

The evaluative phase will follow a quantitative, descriptive design. It is quantitative because the claims under Objective 3 rest on numerical agreement statistics (ICC) and error-rate metrics (PER/WER) rather than interpretive analysis, and descriptive because it characterizes, rather than experimentally manipulates, the application's scoring behavior and on-device performance (latency, memory usage) through summary statistics. Caregiver and SLP usability feedback will be collected using a structured, closed-item questionnaire, keeping the evaluative phase consistently quantitative throughout. Within this phase, agreement testing will be conducted as a within-subjects comparison: the same set of recorded speech attempts will be scored twice, once automatically by EchoVoice and once independently by a licensed SLP who remains blind to the application's output, so that the two sets of scores can be compared directly without either party's judgment influencing the other.

The developmental phase will be carried out iteratively rather than as a single linear build. Because the target vocabulary, exercise structure, and reporting format all depend on clinical input, each major functional module of the system (Manage Exercise, Capture Speech, Speech Recognition and Assessment, Generate Feedback, Monitor Progress, and Configure Goals and Review Assessment) will be developed, internally tested, and revised in short cycles, with the cooperating SLP consulted at defined checkpoints before a module is considered complete. This structure allows clinical feedback gathered early, such as which target words or phonemes are appropriate for the study's autistic child participants, to be incorporated before later, dependent modules are built.

**Materials and Procedures**

	Development** **materials will include a mobile application development framework (e.g., Flutter or React Native) capable of on-device machine learning inference, an on-device ASR runtime (e.g., TensorFlow Lite or ONNX Runtime Mobile) hosting a compressed and quantized version of the Whisper foundation model, following the on-device adaptation approach of Dutta et al. (2025), and a version-controlled codebase maintained throughout the iterative build. The target-word list for listen-and-repeat exercises will be developed and validated in consultation with the partner SLP; consistent with the scope adopted by Sijera et al. (2025) for SamurAI, this word list — and EchoVoice's development and evaluation more broadly — will focus on English-language speech targets, given the greater availability of validated English pronunciation references and assessment frameworks. Expanding language coverage is identified as a direction for future work. Deployment materials will include a small set of consumer-grade Android test devices, spanning entry-level to mid-range specifications, representative of the hardware families realistically available to families in the study area. A clinical scoring rubric (e.g., Percentage of Consonants Correct or an equivalent standardized measure) will serve as the partner SLP's external validation standard. A structured usability questionnaire will be used for caregivers and the SLP, and audio recording and logging tools embedded in the app prototype will capture performance metrics (latency, error rates, resource usage) during testing. The researchers will develop this usability questionnaire specifically for this study, covering the application's interface, exercise flow, and exportable progress report. To establish its content validity, the questionnaire will be reviewed and validated by the thesis adviser and panel members prior to its administration to caregivers and the cooperating SLP, ensuring that its items appropriately measure the usability and acceptability dimensions the study intends to capture. Design documentation will include the system's finalized architectural and functional specifications, developed prior to this stage, which serve as the authoritative blueprint that development work must remain consistent with.

The study will be conducted in partnership with Growth Journey Learning Center Inc. in San Antonio, Agoo, La Union, which will serve as both the source of clinical guidance during development and the site where the application is later evaluated with the center's SLP and the families it serves.

Participants in the evaluative phase of this study will consist of autistic children with speech sound difficulties who are current clients of Growth Journey Learning Center Inc., selected through purposive sampling given the specific clinical profile the study requires. A target sample of XX child participants, aged X–X years old, will be drawn from the center's existing caseload, in coordination with the center's Speech-Language Pathologist. Clinical validation ratings for all participants' recorded speech attempts will be provided by a Speech-Language Pathologist at the partner center, who will serve as the study's external validation standard for Objective 3.

Objective 1 (ASR Model). The researchers will fine-tune and compress a base ASR model using available children's speech corpora and pilot recordings, then convert it for on-device inference. The model will be benchmarked for phoneme error rate, word error rate, and inference latency on the target devices prior to integration into the application.

 Objective 2 (Mobile Interface). The listen-and-repeat interface and the exportable progress-report feature will be developed iteratively, with periodic feedback gathered from the partner SLP and caregivers to refine workflow, content, and usability before finalizing the build.

 Objective 3 (Feasibility and Validation). With informed parental consent and child assent, participating children at the partner center will complete listen-and-repeat sessions using EchoVoice. The same recorded sessions will be independently scored by EchoVoice and by the partner SLP, who will remain blind to the application's output. Device performance (latency, resource usage) will be logged during these sessions to assess real-world feasibility on consumer-grade hardware.*Figure1. Development and Evaluation Procedure of EchoVoice*

The development and evaluation of EchoVoice will proceed through six sequential stages, illustrated above.

Requirements Gathering begins the process, in which the researchers will consult with the cooperating SLP at Growth Journey Learning Center to define therapy goal categories, an appropriate target-word and phoneme list for autistic children with speech sound difficulties, and the content expected in the exportable progress report. This stage anchors every downstream design decision in clinical input rather than researcher assumption.

System Design Verification follows, in which the researchers will cross-check the existing system design documentation, covering data flow, database structure, and modular breakdown, for internal consistency before development begins. Since this documentation serves as the single source of truth for the build, discrepancies caught here prevent costly rework later.

Model Preparation involves selecting and quantizing or compressing the Whisper checkpoint for on-device inference, and constructing the target phoneme dictionary from the SLP-approved word list produced in Stage 1. This stage produces the core recognition component that later modules will depend on.

Iterative Development is the main build stage, in which each of the system's defined functional modules (Manage Exercise, Capture Speech, Speech Recognition and Assessment, Generate Feedback, Monitor Progress, and Configure Goals and Review Assessment) will be built in short cycles, unit-tested against its documented specification, and revised based on periodic SLP checkpoints. This iterative structure lets clinical feedback gathered early be incorporated before later, dependent modules are built.

Internal (System) Testing verifies the system's end-to-end data flow on the target test devices before any child participant is involved, catching integration issues in a controlled environment first.

Field Deployment and Data Collection is the final stage, in which EchoVoice will be deployed to a purposive sample of child participants at the partner center, under caregiver supervision and SLP oversight, to gather the performance, recognition, clinical-validation, and usability data needed for Objective 3.

**Data Gathering**

Four categories of data will be gathered over the course of the study, corresponding to the on-device model, mobile interface, and feasibility/validation dimensions of the study's three specific objectives.

For Objective 1, system performance data will be gathered, consisting of on-device inference latency, memory usage, and processing load per attempt. This data will be logged automatically by the application during internal and field testing on the consumer-grade test devices, and will be used to determine whether the compressed on-device ASR model meets real-time and resource-efficiency requirements. Alongside this, recognition and scoring data will also be gathered for this objective, consisting of the Assessment Records generated by EchoVoice for each practice attempt, namely the predicted phoneme sequence, the phoneme error matrix, and the computed pronunciation score, retrieved directly from the application's Progress Database.

For Objective 2, usability and acceptability data will be gathered through a structured post-use questionnaire administered to caregivers and the cooperating SLP, covering the application's interface, exercise flow, and exportable progress report. This data will be used to assess whether the offline mobile interface and its clinician-ready reporting feature are functional, clear, and useful in practice.

For Objective 3, clinical validation data will be gathered, consisting of independent phoneme- or word-level ratings produced by the cooperating SLP for the same set of recorded attempts already scored by EchoVoice under Objective 1. The SLP will remain blind to the application's automated score for each attempt at the time of rating, ensuring that the two sets of scores are produced independently. This paired data, together with the system performance data gathered under Objective 1, will form the basis for evaluating the application's scoring agreement with clinical judgment and its feasibility on consumer-grade hardware.

**Data Analysis**

	Agreement between EchoVoice's automated scores and the SLP's independent ratings will be measured using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), computed under a two-way, absolute-agreement model. Resulting values will be interpreted using the benchmarks summarized by Taherzadeh Chenani and Madadizadeh (2021), presented in the table below, giving the study a concrete, previously established threshold against which to evaluate Objective 3.

| **ICC Value** | **Level of Agreement** |
| --- | --- |
| Below 0.50 | Poor agreement |
| 0.50 – 0.75 | Moderate agreement |
| 0.75 – 0.90 | Good agreement |
| Above 0.90 | Excellent agreement |

Phoneme Error Rate (PER) and Word Error Rate (WER) will be computed by comparing EchoVoice's predicted phoneme and word sequences against the SLP-verified reference for each attempt, using the standard edit-distance formulation (substitutions, insertions, and deletions divided by the number of reference units). System performance data (latency, memory/CPU usage) will be summarized using descriptive statistics — mean, standard deviation, and range — across devices and sessions, and interpreted against a device-performance threshold informed by benchmarks such as Dorado and Villanueva (2023), to characterize the application's on-device feasibility. Usability and acceptability responses from the structured questionnaire will be summarized using weighted means and frequency counts per item, to surface recurring concerns or preferences among caregivers and the SLP.

Statistical computations will be carried out using Python (with libraries such as pandas and SciPy) or an equivalent statistical software. Where an unfamiliar or less common statistical test is used, its formula and interpretation will be explained in the results chapter.

**Ethical Considerations**

Because this study's participants are autistic children with speech sound difficulties, a population considered especially vulnerable in research, the study will be conducted in accordance with the 2022 National Ethical Guidelines for Research Involving Human Participants (NEGRIHP) issued by the Philippine Health Research Ethics Board (PHREB, 2022), and will be submitted for review and clearance by the appropriate institutional research ethics body before any child participant is involved. Approval and endorsement will likewise be secured from the administration of Growth Journey Learning Center Inc. prior to any data collection at the site.

Because autistic children cannot always give legally recognized informed consent on their own behalf, the study will follow a two-tiered consent process: written informed consent will first be obtained from each child's parent or legal guardian after the study's purpose, procedures, and any foreseeable risks and benefits have been explained in full, and developmentally appropriate assent will separately be sought from the child, respecting the child's own communication style and right to decline or withdraw at any point without penalty (Van Goidsenhoven et al., 2022). Consent and assent will be treated as an ongoing process rather than a single signature; the cooperating SLP and caregiver will continue to check that the child remains willing to participate throughout data collection, and any sign of distress or refusal will end that child's participation immediately. Participation will be voluntary, and families may withdraw at any point without penalty or effect on their continuing therapy at the partner center.

All voice recordings, Assessment Records, and progress reports collected through EchoVoice will be treated as sensitive personal information and handled in accordance with the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). Participants and their families will be briefed that all recordings are processed entirely on-device and will not be transmitted to or stored on any external server, directly addressing the data-privacy concerns identified in the reviewed literature (e.g., Sijera et al., 2025; Samonte et al., 2024). Because the application is designed to run and store data entirely on-device, identifiable recordings and scores will not leave the participant's device or the partner center without explicit parental or guardian authorization; any data extracted for analysis in this study will first be de-identified, with participants referred to by anonymized codes rather than names. Data will be stored securely, accessible only to the research team and the cooperating SLP, and retained only for as long as necessary to complete the study and satisfy institutional archiving requirements.

Finally, the researchers will present all findings, including the agreement statistics computed for Objective 3, honestly and without fabrication, manipulation, or selective omission, and will properly acknowledge every source of information and every prior instrument or model used in the study, in keeping with the standards of academic integrity expected of the research.
